    Saved only in this browser. Height and weight are kept for your reference: no good evidence links them to absorption, so they don’t change the numbers. Ferritin is the most useful thing you can add.

    The short version. For each iron source, the app starts from a base absorption rate, then multiplies it by the blockers and boosters in the same meal or close in time. Each factor has a low and a high value, because studies disagree and single-meal effects are bigger than real-life ones. That gives you a range.

    Iron in your food. Iron per 100 g comes from USDA FoodData Central values, rounded (lentils 3.3 mg, chickpeas 2.9, cooked spinach 3.6, cooked ground beef 2.5, beef liver 4.9). Real foods vary by brand, cut and cooking. Tofu (2.7) is my approximation, and fortified cereal varies hugely, so you enter the iron per 100 g from its label. Beef is treated as all heme iron, which is a simplification.

    Amounts matter. Blockers and boosters are dose dependent: more tea, calcium, phytate or vitamin C changes the result more. Each factor has a reference amount (one cup of coffee, 500 mg of calcium, and so on). At the reference amount you get the full effect in the table below; half the amount gives about half the effect; a much bigger amount adds a little more, up to a limit. Meat boosts plant iron in proportion to the heme iron logged (about 2 mg counts as a standard portion).

    Base rates. Heme iron: 25%. Non-heme iron: depends on ferritin, using a pooled analysis of absorption studies: absorption % = 10(1.82 − 0.73 × log10 ferritin). Ferritin 30 gives about 5.5%, ferritin 15 about 9%, ferritin 50 about 4%. With no ferritin entered the app assumes 30 µg/L, the value European (EFSA) iron recommendations use as their reference.

    Timing. Items in the same meal count fully. With exact times: within 15 minutes counts fully; a blocker after the iron within 90 minutes counts half (one trial found tea 1 hour after a meal blocked about half as much); one before the iron within 60 minutes counts half (weaker evidence). Beyond that, nothing. Meal times come from your Profile.

    Workouts. Hepcidin, the hormone that shuts absorption down, rises for hours after prolonged exercise. Iron eaten 1 to 6 hours after a workout (40+ minutes, or 20+ for intense intervals) is reduced. Evidence is best for endurance running; for strength training it’s weaker, so the reduction is smaller.

    Traffic light and periods. Absorbed iron (middle of the range) is compared with what you lose each day. Everyone loses about 0.8-1 mg a day through skin, gut and urine. Across a whole cycle, periods add on average about 0.56 mg a day (about 15 mg per cycle, very skewed between women; FAO/WHO). Spread over a ~5 day period, that is roughly 3 mg a day, so a period day needs about 4 mg absorbed and every other day about 1 mg. If you choose female in your Profile, a box appears on the Today tab to mark a period day; it isn’t shown for men. Green: at or above the need. Yellow: 60-100%, and on period days anything below the need, because stores cover short gaps and you rebuild over the following days. Red: under 60% on other days. If your ferritin is 50 or higher the light stays neutral. Heavy periods and pregnancy are not modelled: ask a doctor if you think you lose a lot of blood.

    All factors

    Not applied, on purpose

    • Oxalate (spinach) and turmeric: reviews found no effect on absorption.
    • Extra needs from sweat, heavy periods, pregnancy or breastfeeding: no reliable number, so ask a doctor.
    • Long-term adaptation to high-phytate diets: real, but too individual to model.
    • Height and weight: no good evidence for absorption.
